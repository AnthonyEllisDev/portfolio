The idea

This one started as a performance exercise. Naive boids are O(n²) — every fish asks every other fish where it is — and they fall over somewhere around a few hundred agents. I wanted to find out what actually has to change to run thousands, and I wanted the answer visible on screen rather than in a blog post, so the whole thing ships with a live telemetry panel: frame time, fish count, active sand cells, and the number of neighbour checks the spatial grid performed this frame.

Three decoupled systems, one flat state

Physics, AI and rendering never call into each other; they read and write the same typed arrays.

  • Physics — falling-sand cellular automaton over a Uint8Array grid.
  • AI — a structure-of-arrays fish pool steered by a spatial hash.
  • Renderer — water, caustics, sand, fish, overlays, in that order.

The fish pool is a struct-of-arrays with a free list

Position, velocity, tail phase, wiggle rate, palette index and an active flag each live in their own pre-allocated typed array of length 4,000. Spawning pops an index off a free-list stack; killing pushes it back. There are no objects, no garbage, and no allocation during play — spawning 4,000 fish allocates nothing at all, because the memory was there from the first frame. Colour is stored as a one-byte palette index rather than a string, with a small registry mapping indices to hex, RGB and a pre-lightened highlight.

The spatial hash makes flocking linear

Each frame, fish are bucketed into a uniform grid whose cell size equals the perception radius, using a heads/next pair of integer arrays — an intrusive linked list per bucket, rebuilt by overwriting rather than reallocating. A fish then only examines the 3×3 block of buckets around itself. The same structure answers the two interaction queries for free: nearest fish to the cursor for click-to-remove, and all fish inside a rectangle for drag-select. Toggle "show spatial grid" in the demo and you can see the buckets it is actually using.

Sand that is a real cellular automaton

Bottom-up scan; a grain falls if the cell below is empty, otherwise it tries the two diagonals. The scan direction alternates per row and per frame, because a fixed left-to-right sweep makes every pile visibly drift one way — a bias you only notice once you have watched a few thousand grains settle. Sand is drawn in horizontal runs rather than per cell, with a second pass that puts a bright rim on the topmost surface cell of each column, which is what makes a one-byte grid read as a lit surface.

Where the two simulations meet

Fish have to avoid terrain that the player is reshaping in real time — and terrain avoidance is where most flocking demos start doing expensive things. Here it is three O(1) lookups into the sand grid: one probe ahead along the heading, and two perpendicular probes to find the freer side. The fish then steers toward the open side and brakes into the wall. A fish that ends up buried gets a hard upward push, so burying the flock with the Add brush is recoverable rather than fatal.

Boids are usually shown in empty space. Making them share a world with a mutable cellular automaton is the part that forced the sand grid to be cheap to sample — the AI queries it hundreds of times per frame.

Rendering tricks worth stealing

  • One matrix instead of a transform stack. Each fish is drawn with a single setTransform(cos, sin, -sin, cos, x, y), built from the normalised velocity — no save/restore, and no atan2 anywhere in the draw loop.
  • Gradients are cached per colour in local coordinates and reused across every fish of that colour, instead of allocating a gradient per fish per frame.
  • Level of detail by population. Above 700 fish the glow goes; above 1,000 the eyes and dorsal highlight go. Frame rate degrades by dropping detail, not by dropping to a slideshow.
  • Light rays and caustics are drawn with lighter compositing over a vertical water gradient — cheap, and it sells the depth more than any per-pixel effect would.

What I'd do next

  • Water as a second CA layer, so digging a trench actually moves the fish with it.
  • Predators, and a fear term in the steering — the interesting boids behaviour is the panic.
  • Move the flock update to WebGL or a compute-style ping-pong texture and see how far past 4,000 it goes.
  • Touch input, so it works properly on a phone.
