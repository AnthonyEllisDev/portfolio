Arterial — City Traffic Simulator

Draw roads, zone land, and watch a city grow itself — then watch it jam. Congestion here is never a number I chose; it is what happens when a few hundred independently-routed drivers share the network you built.

The idea

City builders usually fake traffic: cars are decoration and congestion is a heuristic applied to a road tile. I wanted the opposite — simulate the drivers and let the city's problems emerge. Every vehicle has a destination, plans its own route, follows the car in front with a real car-following model, decides for itself whether a lane change is worth it, and has to be granted permission to cross a junction. If an avenue backs up, it is because too many of those decisions collided in the same place.

How a car gets from A to B

Roads are Bézier rails, not tiles

Each road is a quadratic Bézier centre line with an arc-length lookup table, so a car's position along it is a single scalar s that maps to a real world point at a constant rate. Lanes are parallel offsets of that curve — shift the endpoints along their normals and re-derive the control point — which means lane geometry, markings, medians and junction pads all fall out of one curve definition. Curvature also caps speed: v ≤ √(a_lat · R), so cars slow into tight bends without any special case.

Routing happens in lane space, not road space

A* runs over lanes and the connectors between them, not over road segments. That is the detail that makes turning behave: a driver who needs to turn left three blocks ahead is already paying for the lane changes in its route cost. The cost function adds a penalty per radian of turn, per lane of lateral movement, and a term proportional to how jammed a lane currently is — so drivers route around congestion rather than politely queueing into it.

Following: the Intelligent Driver Model

Acceleration comes from IDM, using the gap and relative speed to the leader — the standard traffic-flow model, and the reason stop-and-go waves appear on a busy avenue without anything in the code that says "make a wave".

Lane changing: MOBIL

A lane change is accepted only if the total advantage — mine plus a politeness factor times my new follower's loss — clears a threshold, and only if I would not force that follower to brake harder than a safety limit. Plus a small bias toward the kerb lane, so the network self-sorts instead of everyone sitting in the median lane.

Junctions: reservations, not just lights

Every junction pre-computes which of its connectors physically conflict — two turning paths whose rails cross. A car approaching a stop line requests a reservation over the connector it wants; the arbiter grants it only when no conflicting reservation is live. Traffic lights sit on top of this as an extra gate: approaches are clustered into two opposing phases using a doubled-angle trick, so a signal plan is derived from the junction's geometry rather than hand-authored per intersection.

Three problems that were harder than they looked

Roundabouts as a graph rewrite

A roundabout is not a special vehicle behaviour — it is a network mutation. Building one deletes the junction, inserts a ring of nodes, subdivides long spans so each quadratic stays a good circular arc, rewires every arm onto its ring node, and makes the ring edges one-way. After that the existing yielding and reservation logic handles it, because from the simulation's point of view it is just more road. Keeping the physics dumb and the graph smart is what stopped this project from turning into a pile of special cases.

Editing a live network

You can upgrade a street to an avenue, split a road with a new junction, or bulldoze the road a car is currently driving on. Rebuilding an edge replaces its Lane objects, so every vehicle holding a reference has to be re-synced onto the new geometry, every affected node's connector set re-synthesised, and every building's kerb attachment re-resolved. Getting that right — instead of leaking ghost cars and orphaned buildings — was most of the work.

Deadlock, the honest kind

Two failure modes showed up the moment the network got dense. A car stuck at a stop line in the wrong lane would wait forever for a gap that could never come — so it now re-plans a route that starts from the lane it is actually in. And a stalled reservation could hold a junction hostage indefinitely, so reservations expire. Both are cases where the physically correct rule needed an escape hatch to stay playable.

The city on top

Zoning paints a coarse grid; a cell develops only if it can reach a road within a distance band, which is why buildings line the streets you draw instead of appearing in the middle of a block. A building's address is (edge, direction, lane, t) — a point on a lane — so its residents leave from and return to a specific kerb. Homes generate trips to jobs and shops on a timer, freight runs from industry, buildings level up or get abandoned based on how well they are actually being served, and the day/night cycle drives both the lighting and the rhythm of demand.

Determinism where it counts: map generation runs on a seeded mulberry32 PRNG. Reproducible maps make reproducible bugs.

Performance notes

  • Fixed 60 Hz simulation, interpolated rendering — physics never depends on frame rate, and the 2× / 4× speed controls step the sim more times rather than making cars teleport.
  • Geometry is cached, not recomputed. Lane offsets, road markings, median lines and junction pads are built once per edit and reused every frame.
  • Per-lane vehicle indices keep leader/follower lookups local, so car-following does not scan the world.
  • The city in the screenshot above — 35 roads, 115 buildings — holds 58–60 fps with the whole render pipeline on.

What I'd do next

  • Public transport: buses on fixed routes are a good stress test for the reservation system.
  • Adaptive signal timing driven by measured queue length, and a comparison view against the fixed plan.
  • Move routing into a Web Worker — A* is the only thing that can spike a frame.
  • Save/load, so a city worth showing off survives a refresh.
